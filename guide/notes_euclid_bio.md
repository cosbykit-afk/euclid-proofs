## 1. What is actually known vs. what is conjectured (strictly separated)

### KNOWN (SOURCE)
- "only two facts of his life are known, and even these are not beyond dispute." (section: Life and Works)
- Fact 1: "he was intermediate in date between the pupils of Plato (d. 347 b.c.) and Archimedes (b. ca. 287 b.c.)"
- Fact 2: "he taught in Alexandria." Authority: Pappus, who records "that Apollonius spent a long time with the disciples of Euclid in that city" (Pappus, *Collection* VII.35). Note: Pappus's editor Hultsch attributed this passage to an interpolator, but "only for stylistic reasons (and these not very convincing)"; even on that reading the residence is "credible in the light of general probabilities."
- He wrote the *Elements* in thirteen books. (So-called Books XIV and XV are not his: XIV is by Hypsicles, second century b.c.; XV by a pupil of Isidore of Miletus, sixth century.) (SOURCE, per section "Geometry: Elements")
- He is a totally different person from Euclid of Megara, the disciple of Plato, who lived about a hundred years earlier. (SOURCE; article traces the confusion to Valerius Maximus, common in the Middle Ages and in printed editions 1482–1566.)
- Proclus's catalogue of his works (Data, Porisms, Surface Loci, Elements of Music, Divisions, Book of Fallacies, Optics/Phaenomena references) establishes the *titles and survival state* of his works as known to antiquity. (SOURCE)

### CONJECTURED (SOURCE labels these as conjecture or dispute)
- Dating: the once-standard proof that Euclid was older than Archimedes — the citation of *Elements* I.2 in Archimedes' *On the Sphere and the Cylinder* I.2 — was challenged by Johannes Hjelmslev in 1950 as "a naïve interpolation" ("the reference is certainly contrary to ancient practice and is not unfairly characterized as naïve; and although it was already in the text in the time of Proclus, it looks like a marginal gloss which has crept in"). A general survey of the works still places him after pupils of Plato such as Eudoxus and before Archimedes.
- floruit ~295 b.c. (article) or the conventional round 300 b.c. ("no quarrel can be taken with the conventional round date of 300 b.c."); active between ~320 and ~260 b.c.; death "about 270 b.c." — all explicitly conjectural.
- "Lived in the time of the first Ptolemy" is from Proclus, "writing over six centuries later, had no independent knowledge, obviously relying upon Archimedes for his lower date."
- Birthplace unknown; birth date "can only be guessed."
- "It is highly probable... that he attended the Academy, for Athens was the great center of mathematical studies at the time" — stated as probable inference, not fact.
- "He was probably invited to Alexandria when Demetrius of Phalerum, at the direction of Ptolemy Soter, was setting up the great library and museum."
- Platonism: "Euclid may very well have been a Platonist... what Proclus says about his relationship to Plato's associates, Eudoxus and Theaetetus, is borne out by his own works... The testimony of so zealous a Neoplatonist as Proclus is not, however, necessarily conclusive on this point." (SOURCE — the article explicitly keeps this open.)
- Anecdotes: the "no royal road to geometry" reply to Ptolemy (similar to a tale Stobaeus tells of Menaechmus and Alexander); the "Give him three obols" story (Stobaeus); Pappus's character tribute ("most fair and well disposed... although an exact scholar not vaunting himself," aimed at denigrating Apollonius) — the article treats the character assessment as acceptable, the anecdotes as traditional.
- Itard's theory that "Euclid" was a school rather than an individual is reported and rejected: "there is a lack of precise information but no difficulty about Euclid's chronology. No one has hitherto seen any reason for thinking that the author of the *Elements* could not also have been the author of the other books attributed to him."
- Arabic authors' claims about his parentage and life: "either free invention or based on the assumption that the so-called book XIV of the *Elements*, written by Hypsicles, is a genuine work of Euclid."

### SYNTHESIS (not in the article, marked as such)
The article's posture is conservative and source-critical: two hard facts, a securely placed *floruit* window from internal evidence (Euclid quotes Autolycus, postdates Eudoxus/Theaetetus and Aristotle, predates Archimedes and Apollonius's studies with his pupils), and everything else — personality, birthplace, dates of birth and death, even his Platonism — marked probable, plausible, or unverifiable. The guide can use this as a case study in how rigorous inquiry handles the gap between evidence and tradition.

## 2. The *Elements* as a creative organization of existing mathematics

### What the article says Euclid contributed (SOURCE)
Proclus's summary (quoted verbatim from the article's translation of Proclus, *In primum Euclidis*, Friedlein ed. p. 68.6–23):
- "Euclid, who put together the elements, arranging in order many of Eudoxus' theorems, perfecting many of Theaetetus', and also bringing to irrefutable demonstration the things which had been only loosely proved by his predecessors."

The article's own assessment (SOURCE):
- "This is a fair assessment. The *Elements* is on the whole a compilation of things already known, and its most remarkable feature is the arrangement of the matter so that one proposition follows on another in a strictly logical order, with the minimum of assumption and very little that is superfluous."

Proclus's longer assessment, quoted by the article (SOURCE):
- "He deserves admiration preeminently in the compilation of his *Elements of Geometry* on account of the order and selection both of the theorems and of the problems made with a view to the elements. For he included not everything which he could have said, but only such things as were suitable for the building up of the elements... we must make mention of the continuity of the proofs, the disposition and arrangement of the things which precede and those which follow, and the power with which he treats each detail."

### Division of credit: what the article assigns to predecessors vs. Euclid (SOURCE)
- **Eudoxus**: Book V theory of proportion ("Some say that this book is the discovery of Eudoxus, the disciple of Plato" — scholiast; "the arrangement of the book with a view to the elements and the orderly sequence of theorems is recognized by all as the work of Euclid"); the method of exhaustion of Book XII, and specifically the theorems on the volumes of pyramid and cone (XII.7, XII.10), which Archimedes attributes to Eudoxus by name; the proof of XII.2 (circles as squares on diameters) is "also due to Eudoxus."
- **Theaetetus**: the species of irrational lines in Book X (medial, binomial, apotome — credited by a surviving Arabic commentary attributed to Pappus); the underlying work for Book XIII, "the propositions concerning the octahedron and the icosahedron are due to him" (scholiast); but "the credit for the arrangement must again be given to Euclid." The Pappus-commentary verdict quoted: "As for Euclid he set himself to give rigorous rules, which he established, relative to commensurability and incommensurability in general; he made precise the definitions and the distinctions between rational and irrational magnitudes, he set out a great number of orders of irrational magnitudes, and finally he clearly showed their whole extent."
- **Pythagoreans**: the application of areas (I.44–45, II.5–6, 11, 14, VI.27–29) is, per Proclus, "ancient, being discoveries of the muse of the Pythagoreans"; a scholiast to Book IV attributes all sixteen of its problems to them. Discovery of the right-triangle property belongs to Pythagoras or his school.
- **Euclid himself, per the article** (SOURCE):
  - The proof of I.47 ("Pythagoras' theorem"): "it would appear, however, that the famous proof of what is universally known as 'Pythagoras' theorem,' I.47, is due to Euclid himself" — the old proof was based on proportions and therefore not applicable to all magnitudes; Euclid proved it from Book I alone, without invoking similarity/proportions, so it could stand in Book I. Proclus: "I... hold in still greater admiration the writer of the *Elements*, not only because he made it secure by a most clear proof, but because he compelled assent by the irrefutable reasonings of science to the still more general proposition in the sixth book" (VI.31 — similar and similarly situated figures).
  - The parallel postulate: "To Euclid also belongs beyond a shadow of doubt the credit for the parallel postulate which is fundamental to the whole system... In nothing did he show his genius more than in deciding to treat postulate 5 as an indemonstrable assumption."
  - The axiomatic and organizational apparatus: the five postulates and five common notions, the arrangement "with a view to the elements," the formal six-part proposition structure, the selection of only what is "suitable for the building up of the elements," the deliberate restraint of proof technique to each stage (e.g., avoiding similarity in I.47 because a general proportion theory is not yet available at that stage).

### SYNTHESIS
The article's model of Euclid's creativity is organizational and architectonic rather than accumulative: invention of *the system* — what to prove, in what order, from how little — with most individual theorems inherited. His signature creative acts are meta-moves: choosing nonsecancy (non-meeting) as the test of parallelism, deciding the parallel property is an indemonstrable assumption, placing theorems to keep the foundation minimal, and perfecting predecessors' loose proofs into irrefutable demonstrations.

## 3. What was novel about the axiomatic method (SOURCE)

- Each book (or group, XI–XIII) is preceded by definitions of its subject matter; Book I is additionally prefixed with five postulates (αἰτήματα) and five common notions (κοιναὶ ἔννοιαι), "or axioms which are the foundation of the entire work."
- The Aristotelian doctrine behind the setup: "Aristotle had taught that to define an object is not to assert its existence; this must be either proved or assumed." Euclid defines point, straight line, and circle, "then postulates that it is possible" to draw/extend a line and describe a circle — "he assumes the existence of points, straight lines, and circles as the basic elements of his geometry, and with these assumptions he is able to prove the existence of every other figure that he defines" (e.g., the square, defined at I.def.22, is proved to exist at I.46).
- Postulates 1–3 are existential/constructional; each also carries extra content: Postulate 1 implies uniqueness of the line through two points ("two straight lines cannot enclose a space"); Postulate 2 implies uniqueness of the produced part and the infinitude of space; Postulate 3 implies infinitude and continuity of space ("the radius may be indefinitely small").
- Postulate 4 ("All right angles are equal to one another") "is also equivalent to an assumption of the homogeneity of space" — provable only by moving one right angle onto another, which itself assumes invariability of figures; "Euclid prefers to assume that all right angles are equal."
- Postulate 5 (parallels) is treated not as a theorem but as "an unprovable assumption defining the character of one type of space" — revealed as such by Saccheri (1733), Gauss, Lobachevsky (1826, 1829), Bolyai (1832), and Riemann (1854). Ptolemy and Proclus tried to prove it; all such attempts smuggle in an equivalent assumption.
- The common notions are "axioms, which, unlike the postulates, are not confined to geometry but are common to all the demonstrative sciences" (e.g., "Things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another").
- Novelty of the proposition form: each complete proposition has general enunciation (πρότασις), setting-out (ἔκθεσις), definition (διορισμός), construction (κατασκευή), proof (ἀπόδειξις), conclusion (συμπέρασμα), closed by "which was to be proved" (Q.E.D.) / "which was to be done" (Q.E.F.). "These formal divisions of a proposition in such detail are special to Euclid, for Autolycus before him — the only pre-Euclidean author to have any work survive entire — had normally given only a general enunciation and proof... and Archimedes after him frequently omitted the general or particular enunciation."
- Euclid's demonstrations in the *Elements* proceed entirely by synthesis (from the known to the unknown), "and nowhere is appeal made to analysis" — though *reductio ad absurdum* is used frequently and "may be regarded as a form of analysis." "No hint is given by Euclid about the way in which he first realized the truth of the propositions that he proves. Majestically he proceeds by rigorous logical steps from one proved proposition to another, using them like steppingstones, until the final goal is reached."
- Aristotle's influence is confirmed by the postulates/common notions ("Euclid's postulates and axioms or 'common notions' undoubtedly show the influence of Aristotle's elaborate discussion of these topics"), while Aristotle himself shows no awareness of Euclid (he cites a pre-Euclidean proof of I.5).

### SYNTHESIS
The article's novelty claims are: (1) the existence–definition split (define, then postulate existence, then prove existence of everything else); (2) the postulates/common notions split (geometry-specific vs. universal); (3) the complete formal proposition template; (4) strict synthesis; (5) the strategic choice to treat parallelism as an indemonstrable assumption — the article calls this his greatest genius. It does not claim Euclid invented deduction or analysis itself (both discussed by Aristotle and Pappus); the invention is the architectural discipline of a single foundation carrying thirteen books.

## 4. The commentary tradition and Euclid's intent (SOURCE)

### Who the commentators are
- "Euclid's *Elements* was the subject of commentaries in antiquity by Hero, Pappus, Porphyry, Proclus, and Simplicius; and Geminus had many observations about it in a work now lost."
- Proclus's commentary on Book I (*In primum Euclidis*, 5th c. A.D.) is the principal source: it contains the summary of the history of geometry with the Euclid passage, the definitions of elements/letters analogy ("elements" likened to the letters of the alphabet), and the full assessment of Euclid's achievement.
- Pappus: records the Alexandria residence, includes Euclid's *Data*, *Porisms*, *Surface Loci* in the "Treasury of Analysis" (τóπος ἀναλυόμενος — Gow's "storehouse (or treasury) of analysis"), and describes the *Data* as "a collection of hints on analysis"; he exonerates Euclid on the conics ("he wrote so much about the locus as was possible by means of the *Conics* of Aristaeus but did not claim finality for his proofs").
- Marinus of Naples (pupil and biographer of Proclus): wrote a commentary/introduction to the *Data* on the senses of "given."
- Theon of Alexandria (4th c.): "reëdited" the *Elements*, "altering the language in some places with a view to greater clarity, interpolating intermediate steps, and supplying alternative proofs, separate cases, and corollaries." All manuscripts known until the 19th century derived from Theon's recension; then Peyrard's Vatican manuscript (*P*) "obviously gives an earlier text and is the basis of Heiberg's definitive edition."

### What the tradition says about Euclid's intent
- Proclus on the title: "elements" are "leading theorems having to those which follow the character of an all-pervading principle"; likened to the letters of the alphabet (the Greek word is the same).
- Proclus's famous verdict on intent: "In his aim he was a Platonist, being in sympathy with this philosophy, whence he made the end of the whole *Elements* the construction of the so-called Platonic figures." (SOURCE quotation; the article then cautions: Proclus was "so zealous a Neoplatonist" that his testimony is "not necessarily conclusive"; the article's own view is that "since he shows the influence of Plato in other ways, this splendid ending could easily be a grain of incense at the Platonic altar.")
- On Book X, Pappus's commentary (via Arabic, attributed to Pappus by Heiberg) gives intent: Euclid "set himself to give rigorous rules... he made precise the definitions and the distinctions between rational and irrational magnitudes... and finally he clearly showed their whole extent."
- On the *Porisms*: Pappus says Euclid "appears in all the porisms to have laid down only the first principles and seminal ideas of the many important matters investigated."
- On the *Book of Fallacies* (lost; known from Proclus): it is "purgative and disciplinary, while the Elements contains an irrefutable and complete guide to the actual scientific investigation of geometrical matters."
- On the *Data*: the concept is "that if certain things are given, other things are necessarily implied, until we are brought to something that is agreed" — the *Data* is "a collection of hints on analysis."
- A 1967 scholarly aside quoted in the article's notes (Zeuthen via van der Waerden): "Euclid is by no means a great mathematician... Euclid is first of all a pedagogue, not a creative genius. It is very difficult to say which original discoveries Euclid added to the work of his predecessors" — the article's main text rejects this by the evidence of the parallel postulate and I.47.

## 5. How the article frames Euclid's significance for human reason (SOURCE)

- "Euclid's fame rests preeminently upon the *Elements*... which has exercised an influence upon the human mind greater than that of any other work except the Bible." Hence he became known in antiquity as "the Writer of the Elements" (ὁ Στοιχειωτής) and simply "the Geometer" (ὁ Γεωμέτρης); the article's first paragraph adds that his name "became a synonym for geometry until the twentieth century."
- The twofold significance (SOURCE, exact wording):
  1. "In the first place, it introduced into mathematical reasoning new standards of rigor which remained throughout the subsequent history of Greek mathematics and, after a period of logical slackness following the revival of mathematics, have been equaled again only in the past two centuries."
  2. "In the second place, it marked a decisive step in the geometrization of mathematics." The article argues that Greek mathematics "might have developed in other directions" (Pythagoreans, Democritus, Archimedes in some works, Diophantus), but "it was Euclid in his *Elements*, possibly under the influence of that philosopher who inscribed over the doors of the Academy 'God is for ever doing geometry,' who ensured that the geometrical form of proof should dominate mathematics."
- That influence is "reflected in two of the supreme works in the history of thought, Newton's *Principia* and Kant's *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*": Newton's work "is cast in the form of geometrical proofs that Euclid had made the rule even though Newton had discovered the calculus, which would have served him better and made him more easily understood by subsequent generations"; Kant's belief in the universal validity of Euclidean geometry "led him to a transcendental aesthetic which governs all his speculations on knowledge and perception."
- The Popper note quoted in the article (SOURCE): "Ever since Plato and Euclid, but not before, geometry (rather than arithmetic) appears as the fundamental instrument of all physical explanations and descriptions, in the theory of matter as well as in cosmology."
- The long reign: "It was only toward the end of the nineteenth century that the spell of Euclidean geometry began to weaken and that a desire for the 'arithmetization of mathematics' began to manifest itself; and only in the second quarter of the twentieth century, with the development of quantum mechanics, have we seen a return in the physical sciences to a neo-Pythagorean view of number as the secret of all things. Euclid's reign has been a long one; and although he may have been deposed from sole authority, he is still a power in the land."
- The fifth postulate as an intellectual hinge: Saccheri in 1733 "first asked himself what would be the consequences of hypotheses other than that of Euclid, and in so doing he stumbled upon the possibility of non-Euclidean geometries"; Gauss, Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann then made Euclid's assumption "revealed for what it really is — an unprovable assumption defining the character of one type of space."

### SYNTHESIS
The article presents Euclid's significance as institutional and epistemic, not just mathematical: he made geometry the *form of proof* for two millennia — the template Newton was still using two thousand years later — and the price of that template (the fifth postulate) became, two thousand years later, the doorway to non-Euclidean space. The guide can draw a direct lesson from this: stating an assumption explicitly (rather than smuggling it in, as the postulate-provers did) is what turns a constraint into a discovery.
