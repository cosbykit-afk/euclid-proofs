## 1. How it defines creative thinking in mathematics (SOURCE)

- Section 1, "Creativity in learning mathematics": creativity in learning is
  partly about "allowing students to enjoy learning more and think for
  themselves", and partly vocational — future jobs will rely less on
  mechanistic work (done by computers) and more on "problem solving, thinking
  outside the box and coming up with creative solutions".
- Its theoretical lens is creativity as **"possibility thinking"** (citing
  Aristeidou, 2011; Craft et al., 2012; Grainger et al., 2007): using "What if?"
  scenarios. The paper lists five classroom features of possibility thinking:
  **posing questions, experimenting with ideas, taking risks, playing around,
  and working collaboratively**.
- Section 2, "The role of choice": playfulness supports creativity because play
  is spontaneous exploration of many possible solutions — this is named
  **divergent thinking**; play means exploring and experimenting, for any age.
- Section 3: the operational form of possibility thinking in mathematics is
  "What happens if …?" thinking — thinking about changes in situations, which
  works with variables in mathematics and reveals the roles of variables vs
  constants ("'What will happen to the other variables when I change this
  variable?'").
- Section 5, Summary: "Possibility thinking asks students to be creative, to
  try things out and make their own decisions – and therefore their own
  mistakes." Teachers sometimes think their job is to prevent mistakes; "This
  unit demonstrates that it is the teacher's job to allow the students to make
  mistakes and to learn from them." Playing with ideas means "they exercise
  their creative side, try out lots of ideas and so, end up really knowing and
  understanding what they have learned."

## 2. Its learning cycle (SOURCE; not Polya's four phases by name)

Polya (1957) *How to Solve It* and Polya (1962) *Mathematical Discovery* appear
only in the bibliography (References page). The four-phase "understand, plan,
carry out, look back" cycle is not stated in the body. The paper's own
recurring cycle, reconstructed from Activities 1–3 and the case studies, is:

1. **Play / explore freely with choices** — e.g. "In how many ways can you
   divide each of the shapes into right-angled triangles?" (Activity 1,
   §2), with "the choice to approach a problem in different ways, the option
   to make mistakes or the choice to come up with their own conjectures".
2. **Predict before testing** — Activity 2: "first think about what is going to
   happen before testing their ideas"; "first write down what you think will
   be the change; then check by drawing the changes". In Part 2 the paper goes
   further: fill in what will happen "without any drawing at all – even in
   their mind's eye!" and only then draw to check.
3. **Make your own conjectures and test them** — §2: "come up with their own
   conjectures and test whether they are valid or not"; §3: students gain
   "ownership and feeling valued for their thinking powers from coming up with
   their own conjectures and by using their own examples to work on."
4. **Collate and generalise** — §3: "collating information based on these
   different examples will also allow for generalisations to be made."
5. **Notice and ask why** — recurring prompt "What do you notice? Why do you
   think this is?" (Activities 2, §3); case study 2 ends with class discussion
   of what was noticed.
6. **Meta-cognitive reflection** — §3: thinking about "what thinking is
   required" is called "meta-cognition"; §5 claims creativity leaves students
   "better placed when asked about something in an unfamiliar context…
   They know that if they think around an idea and try some things out, they
   can solve a problem that looks difficult to begin with – just as they have
   done before."

## 3. How it teaches deduction and proof (SOURCE)

- The unit framing (p. 1): "Trigonometry plays a very important role in the
  Indian National Curriculum Framework (2005). It links concepts about shape
  and space with other mathematical ideas such as ratio, deduction and
  mathematical proof."
- §3 frames Activity 2 ("What happens if …?") as the route into deductive
  reasoning: varying a side/angle of a right-angled triangle and completing
  tables of increase/decrease leads students to discover functional
  relationships, and "the role of and connection between, variables and
  constants are also discovered" — the groundwork of ratio.
- Activity 2 Part 2 deliberately builds toward the trigonometric constants: a
  table of right-angled triangles with fixed angle C (90°) and proportional
  sides (AB = 2,4,6,8 with AC = 1,2,3,4), asking "What do you notice? Why do
  you think this is?" — i.e. students discover the angle→ratio correspondence
  themselves rather than being given the sine/cosine definitions.
- Proof-style justification is requested explicitly: "Do you think that all
  closed polygons can be formed by right angled triangles? Give reasons to
  justify your answer." (Activity 1). §2 notes the play with right-angled
  triangles becomes "a tool to 'getting unstuck' later" when proving the
  cosine rule.
- Deductive structure is taught through writing explanations: Activity 3 asks
  students to design a clinometer manual "to explain to its users how the
  clinometer works and how to use it", including "mathematical explanations on
  how and why a clinometer can be used for working out heights" — i.e. proving
  why a method works, in writing, for a knowledgeable audience.
- NCF/NCFTE Resource 1: "View learning as a search for meaning out of personal
  experiences and knowledge generation as a continuously evolving process of
  reflective learning"; "Support students to learn to enjoy mathematics rather
  than fear it."

## 4. What learner activities it counts as creative (SOURCE)

- Posing and pursuing "What if?" / "What happens if …?" questions
  (Activities 1–3).
- Playing, exploring, experimenting with geometric figures — e.g. dividing
  polygons into right-angled triangles, and the reverse: "Draw any
  right-angled triangle… What kind of closed polygons can you construct by
  using this triangle as a building block?" (Activity 1; case study 1 notes
  students making hexagons, random formations, even figurines).
- Mental visualisation: a student "increasing the angle in my mind's eye so I
  can 'see' what happens to the other sides" (Case Study 2, Mr Chadha) — the
  paper treats this "mind's eye" work as legitimate mathematical activity
  before drawing.
- Making, choosing, and owning mistakes; self-correction without teacher
  interference first ("I decided not to interfere immediately, as I would
  normally do, but allow the students to make their own mistakes!" — Case
  Study 1, Mrs Nagaraju; students self-corrected by glancing at peers' work
  and re-reading the question).
- Inventing conjectures and testing them against drawings, with tolerance for
  conjectures proving incorrect — which "can also surprise them and make them
  feel intrigued about 'Why it is that …?'" (§3).
- Collaborative refinement: "first exploring possibilities on their own, and
  then discussing these with classmates or in groups to get more ideas and
  refine their thinking" (§2); the "think 30 seconds → pair → pairs to fours →
  class" discussion routine in Case Study 2.
- Designing a communication product: writing the clinometer user manual with
  mathematical explanations (Activity 3).
- Generalising from collated examples and asking "What do you notice? Why do
  you think this is?"

## 5. Explicit claims linking creativity with rigor (SOURCE)

- Intro (p. 1): "if you make small changes to tasks, students will be able to
  learn more effectively. When students are allowed to make more choices and
  decisions themselves, they can enjoy trigonometry and feel empowered by
  their mathematics learning." — creativity-as-choice improves effectiveness,
  not just enjoyment.
- §2: the playful skill (dividing polygons into right-angled triangles) is
  framed as a rigorous enabler — being able to "just do it" when right-angled
  triangles must be found later "such as when proving the cosine rule"; play
  becomes "a tool to 'getting unstuck'".
- §3: "Activity 2 asks students to think about asking 'What happens if I
  change …?'" — prediction-first discipline: "first write down what you think
  will be the change; then check by drawing the changes"; Case Study 2
  sharpens it into theory-vs-practice adjudication: "I asked them to think
  whether it was their 'theory' that was likely to be 'wrong', or their
  'practice'. They quickly understood what I was saying and were able to argue
  for their own 'theory'." — i.e. creative conjecture kept honest by
  disciplined checking.
- §5: creative play is explicitly tied to durable understanding and
  examination transfer: "Asking the students to 'play with ideas' means that
  they exercise their creative side, try out lots of ideas and so, end up
  really knowing and understanding what they have learned… they are better
  placed when asked about something in an unfamiliar context, as often happens
  in examinations."
- Case Study 3 (Mrs Meganathan) on the teacher side: step-by-step tasks "tell
  them what to do and how to think, so there is little choice or opportunity
  for creativity"; removing steps forces students to "figure that out for
  themselves" — a creative task is a more demanding one, not a looser one.
- NCF framing: shift "away from rote methods" while keeping the curricular
  demand (shape/space + ratio + deduction + proof) — creativity is the means
  to the rigorous end, not an alternative to it.

## SYNTHESIS (for the guide drafters)

- The paper's model can be compressed for the guide as: **choose → vary →
  predict → test → generalise → reflect**, with "What happens if …?" as the
  generative question and conjecture-then-check as the rigor mechanism.
  This is SYNTHESIS (the paper never names the cycle as one sequence).
- For the Euclid-parallel in the guide: Activity 2's conjecture→check→
  generalise sequence closely mirrors the structure of a Euclidean
  proposition (figure, statement, deduction, conclusion); the guide can
  present Euclid's proposition format as the formal version of the paper's
  informal cycle. SYNTHESIS.
